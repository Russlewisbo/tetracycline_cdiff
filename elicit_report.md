Among the studies reporting protective effects, doxycycline consistently demonstrated reduced CDI risk. Doernberg et al. found that for each day of doxycycline receipt, the rate of CDI was 27% lower than in patients not receiving doxycycline when both groups received ceftriaxone (HR 0.73, 95% CI: 0.56-0.96)[S. Doernberg et al., 2012]. Similarly, John et al. reported an 85% reduced incidence of CDI with ceftriaxone-doxycycline compared to ceftriaxone-azithromycin (RR 0.15, 95% CI: 0.03-0.75)[J. John et al., 2018]. Baxter et al. found doxycycline was associated with a significantly reduced risk of hospital-acquired CDI (OR 0.41)[R. Baxter et al., 2008].

Tigecycline also demonstrated protective effects. Bartoletti et al. found tigecycline-based regimens for intra-abdominal infections were associated with a 10-fold lower incidence of CDI compared to meropenem-based regimens (HR 0.07, 95% CI: 0.03-0.71)[M. Bartoletti et al., 2018].

Two studies reported contrasting findings. Teng et al., analyzing FAERS adverse event reports, found tetracyclines had a reporting odds ratio of 7.54 (95% CI: 5.42-10.50)[Chengwen Teng et al., 2019], indicating increased reporting of CDI as an adverse event. However, this disproportionality analysis differs methodologically from clinical studies and may reflect reporting patterns rather than true incidence. Cui et al. identified tetracycline as an independent risk factor for CDI with an odds ratio of 22.459[Yihong Cui et al., 2023], though this study was conducted in China and may reflect different prescribing patterns or C. difficile strain epidemiology.

Several large studies provided narrative findings without specific effect estimates. Webb et al., analyzing over 500,000 admissions, found doxycycline and daptomycin were associated with lower CDI risk compared to most other antibiotics[B. Webb et al., 2020; B. Webb et al., 2020]. O'Leary et al. reported a 17% decreased risk of CDI with doxycycline compared to azithromycin when used with ceftriaxone for pneumonia treatment (P=0.03), with the protective effect being particularly pronounced in patients with prior CDI history (45% reduction, OR 0.55, P=0.02)[Ashley L O'Leary et al., 2023].

Zhang et al., examining outpatient antibiotic prescriptions, used doxycycline as the reference comparator and found that clindamycin (aOR 8.81, 95% CI: 7.76-10.00), cefdinir (aOR 5.86, 95% CI: 5.03-6.83), cefuroxime (aOR 4.57, 95% CI: 3.87-5.39), and fluoroquinolones (aOR 4.05, 95% CI: 3.58-4.59) all conferred substantially higher CDI risk than doxycycline[Jimmy Zhang et al., 2022]. Boven et al., in a Swedish population-based study, found no association between tetracyclines and CDI[A. Boven et al., 2026; A. Boven et al., 2026].

Miller et al. ranked antibiotics from least to most likely to cause CDI and placed minocycline and doxycycline among those with the lowest risk, while clindamycin had the highest[A. Miller et al., 2023]. Katz et al. reported the lowest CA-CDI rates following penicillin, doxycycline, and azithromycin[M. Katz et al., 2017]. Stevens et al. reported that tetracycline exposure appeared protective in a national Veterans Affairs cohort[V. Stevens et al., 2018]. Young et al., in another national VA study, found tetracyclines were negatively associated with CDI (OR 0.58, 95% CI: 0.51-0.66), while macrolides also showed negative association (OR 0.83, 95% CI: 0.77-0.91)[Eric H. Young et al., 2019; Eric H. Young et al., 2019].

In pediatric populations, Dang et al. reported that incident rates of CA-CDI were highest following clindamycin, cephalosporins, and amoxicillin-clavulanate, with lower rates following amoxicillin, azithromycin, and other agents[Rebecca Dang et al., 2021]. Adams et al., in another pediatric study, found clindamycin (OR 73.00, 95% CI: 13.85-384.68) and third-generation cephalosporins (OR 16.32, 95% CI: 9.11-29.26) were most strongly associated with CA-CDI[Daniel J. Adams et al., 2017].

Studies examining comparator antibiotics consistently identified clindamycin, cephalosporins (particularly third-generation), fluoroquinolones, and carbapenems as high-risk agents[P. Stojanović et al., 2022; B. Webb et al., 2020; Jimmy Zhang et al., 2022; A. Miller et al., 2023; R. Baxter et al., 2008; Chengwen Teng et al., 2019; M. Hensgens et al., 2012; Eric H. Young et al., 2019; K. Brown et al., 2016; K. Brown et al., 2020; A. Guh et al., 2015; S. Dial et al., 2008; Daniel J. Adams et al., 2017; Quentin Minson & Steve Mok, 2007]. These comparisons consistently positioned tetracyclines, particularly doxycycline, as lower-risk alternatives.

Comparative studies of community-acquired pneumonia treatment provided clinically relevant context. Reeves et al. found no significant differences in in-hospital mortality, clinical failure, readmission rates, or hospital length of stay between doxycycline plus β-lactam versus azithromycin plus β-lactam or respiratory fluoroquinolone monotherapy[S D Reeves et al., 2024]. Similarly, Greco Kinney et al. found no significant differences in mortality or secondary outcomes between azithromycin and doxycycline groups when both were combined with β-lactam therapy for severe CAP[Alexandra Greco Kinney et al., 2023]. These findings suggest that doxycycline can serve as an effective alternative without compromising clinical outcomes while potentially reducing CDI risk.

## Synthesis of Heterogeneous Findings

The apparent contradiction between studies showing protective effects of tetracyclines and those showing increased risk or no association can be explained through several mechanisms.

First, the distinction between **tigecycline and other tetracyclines** is critical. Tigecycline has broader spectrum activity and different pharmacokinetics compared to doxycycline and minocycline. Bartoletti et al.'s finding that tigecycline reduced CDI risk compared to meropenem[M. Bartoletti et al., 2018] must be interpreted in the context that tigecycline itself may have anti-C. difficile activity[Yihong Cui et al., 2023; W. Dai et al., 2020], whereas doxycycline's protective effect likely reflects less disruption of normal gut flora. Cui et al.'s finding that tetracycline and tigecycline were risk factors[Yihong Cui et al., 2023] may reflect the specific clinical contexts in which tigecycline is used (critically ill patients with resistant infections) rather than an inherent property of the drug.

Second, **methodological differences** explain much of the apparent heterogeneity. Teng et al.'s FAERS analysis[Chengwen Teng et al., 2019; Chengwen Teng et al., 2019] measured adverse event reporting rather than clinical incidence. FAERS data are subject to reporting bias—physicians may be more likely to report unexpected adverse events (CDI with tetracyclines) than expected ones (CDI with clindamycin), inflating the apparent association. This contrasts with clinical cohort and case-control studies that measure actual CDI incidence.

Third, **geographic and temporal variation** in C. difficile epidemiology affects findings. Studies from China[Yihong Cui et al., 2023; W. Dai et al., 2020] and Serbia[P. Stojanović et al., 2022; Stojanovic Predrag, 2016] may reflect different strain distributions, with ST3 being most frequent in Chongqing[Yihong Cui et al., 2023] and RT001 and RT027 predominant in Serbia[P. Stojanović et al., 2022]. The protective effect of tetracyclines may vary by strain susceptibility. However, even in the Serbian study by Stojanović et al., tetracyclines showed a protective association (OR 0.146, 95% CI: 0.07-0.22)[P. Stojanović et al., 2022], suggesting the protective effect is robust across different strain distributions.

Fourth, **exposure timing and definition** varies across studies. Studies examining antibiotic exposure within 30 days[A. Boven et al., 2026; A. Miller et al., 2023; Kevin A. Brown et al., 2026; Nele Moreels et al., 2024], 60 days[R. Baxter et al., 2008], or 90 days[Jimmy Zhang et al., 2022; K. Brown et al., 2020; K. Kavanagh et al., 2016] of CDI diagnosis capture different risk windows. Kavanagh et al. demonstrated that CDI risk is greatest with recent high-risk antibiotic exposure (OR 12.5, 95% CI: 8.9-17.4 in first month) but remains elevated 4-6 months later (OR 2.6, 95% CI: 1.7-3.9)[K. Kavanagh et al., 2016]. Studies with longer exposure windows may dilute protective effects if tetracyclines are prescribed earlier in the observation period.

Fifth, **indication and combination therapy** patterns matter. Studies examining pneumonia treatment[Ashley L O'Leary et al., 2023; S. Doernberg et al., 2012; S D Reeves et al., 2024; J. John et al., 2018; Alexandra Greco Kinney et al., 2023] found protective effects when doxycycline was combined with ceftriaxone or other β-lactams. The protective effect may result from avoiding higher-risk alternatives (macrolides, fluoroquinolones) rather than an inherent protective property of doxycycline. However, even accounting for ceftriaxone exposure, doxycycline independently reduced CDI risk[S. Doernberg et al., 2012], suggesting a true protective mechanism.

Sixth, **baseline risk stratification** affects apparent associations. Boven et al.'s finding of no association for tetracyclines[A. Boven et al., 2026] in a population-based Swedish study may reflect that tetracyclines are prescribed to patients at lower baseline CDI risk. In contrast, tigecycline studies[M. Bartoletti et al., 2018; W. Dai et al., 2020] examine critically ill patients at inherently higher CDI risk. When Brown et al. examined nursing home residents—a high-risk population—they found moxifloxacin resulted in 121% more risk than amoxicillin (ARR 2.21, 95% CI: 1.67-3.08), while tetracycline duration effects were measurable[K. Brown et al., 2020].

Finally, **dose-response relationships** provide mechanistic insight. Webb et al. found that for every antibiotic day of therapy prior to admission, the odds of subsequent CDI increased by 12.8% (95% CI: 12.2-13.4%), a much stronger association than inpatient antibiotic exposure (OR 1.007, 95% CI: 1.005-1.009)[B. Webb et al., 2020]. This cumulative effect was less pronounced for doxycycline, consistent with a protective or neutral effect. Brown et al. demonstrated that longer antibiotic duration was associated with increased CDI risk: 10-day and 14-day courses incurred 12% (ARR 1.12, 95% CI: 1.09-1.14) and 27% (ARR 1.27, 95% CI: 1.21-1.30) more risk compared to 7-day courses[K. Brown et al., 2020].

Integrating these findings, the evidence supports that **doxycycline and minocycline consistently show protective or neutral associations with CDI across most settings and populations**. The apparent protective effect is strongest when doxycycline is compared directly to high-risk antibiotics (clindamycin, cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones) and appears independent of combination therapy. **Tigecycline** shows context-dependent effects that may reflect both patient selection and potential anti-C. difficile activity. The few studies showing increased risk are explicable by methodological differences (FAERS reporting bias), specific geographic contexts, or conflation of tetracycline-class agents with different properties.

For clinical practice, these findings support using doxycycline as a preferred agent when tetracycline coverage is indicated, particularly for community-acquired pneumonia[Ashley L O'Leary et al., 2023; S. Doernberg et al., 2012; S D Reeves et al., 2024; J. John et al., 2018; Alexandra Greco Kinney et al., 2023] or as an alternative to fluoroquinolones and clindamycin in patients at high CDI risk[Jimmy Zhang et al., 2022; A. Miller et al., 2023; K. Brown et al., 2020]. The protective effect is most clinically meaningful in populations with elevated baseline CDI risk, including older adults[Jimmy Zhang et al., 2022], those with recent healthcare exposure[B. Webb et al., 2020; K. Kavanagh et al., 2016], and patients with prior CDI[Ashley L O'Leary et al., 2023].
